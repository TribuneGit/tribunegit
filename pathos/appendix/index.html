Appendix · At a glance

The thread, at a glance

A future with AI that doesn't leave the human behind. It hands the hours back, so the people at Pathos can spend them on the touch points that made Pathos in the first place: the idea, the craft, the relationship.

The goal

Make Pathos more efficient, without losing what makes it Pathos. The same people, with more of the week for the work only a human can do, a way of working that stays when people and tools change, and pricing that rewards the outcome rather than the hours.

What we heard

Human touch points.The repetitive work can go. The idea, the judgment and the relationship stay with the person.
Better, not fewer.More reach for every person, nobody replaced, and pace with the agencies already using AI well.
No tool carousel.Stay current without the team relearning a new tool every other month.
Knowledge that stays.A way of working stays in the house for whoever comes next.
Say it before you build it.Requirements first, then delivery, agreed before the first hour of work.
Thirty hours, three hours.When the work takes a tenth of the time, what does the invoice say?

The thesis

Process over person.How Pathos works is written down per process, so it belongs to the agency and stays when someone moves on.
Capability over tool.Each process names the kind of help it needs. The tool is a field you can change.
Value over hours.The client pays for what the work makes possible. The hour becomes an internal number.

Efficiency here is counted in hours given back to people, never in people removed. AI takes the first pass at the repetitive parts of the week: first drafts, versions, resizes, research and reporting.

The team spends the time it saves on ideas, craft and clients, so Pathos can take on more work with the same people before it needs to hire.

  • Human touch points: decided per process, with the team: what stays with a person, what goes to AI first.
  • Better, not fewer: more capacity per person, counted in hours back each week.
  • No tool carousel: the Automation Map, note 8.
  • Knowledge that stays: the Process Registry and training, notes 7 and 12.
  • Say it before you build it: the two documents, note 11.
  • Thirty hours, three hours: value over hours, note 5.

Take client onboarding. Today the best way to run it may live with one experienced account lead. Written down as a process, with the steps, the templates and that lead's own notes, a new colleague can run it in their first week.

The lead's way of working stays in the house, and the lead can keep refining it for everyone.

A process says what kind of help it needs, for example "a first draft of the creative brief", not which app to open. The tool that does it today is one line in a list.

When a better tool comes along, only that line changes. The process, the prompts and the training around it stay the same, so nobody has to relearn their job.

If a campaign concept took 30 hours and now takes 3, billing 3 hours gives the gain away. Pricing the outcome keeps it: a fixed package for a launch, a campaign season or a brand refresh.

The client notices faster answers and more directions to choose from. The hours become an internal number that tells Pathos its margin.

In a survey of about 200 US agency leaders, 87% said they use generative AI and 81% use it to make their teams more productive. Yet 61% still count it as a cost of doing business, and only 6% have made it part of what they sell.

Most agencies use AI quietly, inside the building. Very few have turned it into an offer clients can see and buy.

Source: The State of AI Inside US Marketing Agencies 2026, Forrester with the 4As, April 2026.

A shared, living record of how work gets done at Pathos, one entry per process. Each entry names who owns it, what starts it, the steps, what comes out at the end, and how much of it is automated.

Example: client onboarding starts when a contract is signed and runs through the kickoff call, a structured brief, scope and estimate, and client sign-off. It is written with the people who do the work, and it is worth having even for processes that never get automated.

For each process, a short list of the kinds of help it uses, such as drafting, research, reporting or media, and which tool fills each one today. Because the process names the job and not the product, a tool can be swapped without rewriting the work.

OpenClaw, the AI orchestration platform we work with, is the layer that connects those tools and runs on hardware Pathos owns. It is built to stay for years, and built so that even it could be replaced without starting over.

An internal record, per process, of what it took to set up, what it costs to run each month, how many hours it saves, and whether quality held.

It is filled in only once a process is written down and running, so the numbers describe real work rather than estimates. It tells Pathos its margin and which processes are worth expanding. Clients never see it.

When someone finds a better way to do a task, such as a prompt, a shortcut or a new tool, they suggest it for that process instead of quietly doing it their own way.

A named person looks at it. If it works, it becomes the new standard, with a note on who suggested it and why. If not, it is kept on file with the reason, so good ideas reach everyone and nothing is argued twice.

Two short documents, agreed before any work starts.

The Requirements Book is written by whoever needs the work: what has to be achieved and why, what "done" looks like, and the limits of time, budget and taste.

The Delivery Book is written by whoever does the work: how it will be done, by whom, in which steps, and how everyone will know it is finished.

Between Tribune and Pathos, Pathos's needs shape the first and we write the second. Between Pathos and a client, the client's brief becomes the Requirements Book and Pathos's proposal becomes the Delivery Book. The client signs off on outcomes rather than hours, and any later change is agreed in writing.

Training belongs to the process, not to a person or a tool. It lives in two places: a learning space with short videos and walkthroughs, in the style of LearnWorlds or Skool, and a Pathos knowledge base where each process, its history and the team's notes are kept.

People learn a process, run it, write back what they would change, and the good changes become the new standard. A new hire starts from what the team already knows.

A few hours with each team to watch a normal week of work, and a conversation with Alex about the tools and systems already in place.

From that, we choose a first target together, write the Requirements and Delivery Books for it, and agree on scope and pricing before anything is set up.

Starting with one department gives real results quickly without spreading thin. Each person in that team gets an OpenClaw assistant, set up with them for their role, for example one that turns kickoff call notes into a first draft of the brief.

The ledger tracks what changes. After about three months, those results decide what comes next.

Once one department runs well, the same approach moves to the next, faster each time because the registry, the tools and the training already exist.

At that point a second brain for leadership becomes possible: an AI assistant that prepares research before meetings, lays out options with their costs, and shows what is on track across departments. Ryan runs his own company this way. The decisions stay with Shane and the department heads.
